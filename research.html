Future Proves Past: Detecting Hidden Fraud under Imperfect Enforcement Labels

Accounting-fraud detection faces a two-sided problem created by imperfect enforcement labels. Models trained on SEC enforcement actions may learn the enforcement process rather than the fraud process, because observed AAER cases are only a selectively revealed subset of fraudulent firms; and if labels are incomplete, classification accuracy cannot tell whether a model finds hidden fraud or merely reproduces enforcement patterns.

We address both with a future-proves-past framework that folds future accounting outcomes into the supervisory signal, implemented as a deep convolutional neural network trained on multi-year accounting panels with soft labels. The model holds up on traditional SEC-labelled benchmarks, but its main contribution is identifying economically meaningful hidden fraud outside the enforcement set: high-risk firms show higher future equity issuance, substantial post-issuance underperformance, and significant negative abnormal returns, while independently verified clean populations receive systematically lower fraud rankings.

with C. Wei Li, Yuxuan Li, and Anand M. Vijh

Listening to History Rhymes: Pattern Recurrence and Return Predictability in Stock Markets

We embed rolling 60-day return segments of U.S. equities from 1970 to 2024 — roughly 1.32 million segments — into a geometric space of standardized trajectories, extending the exhaustive pairwise-comparison principle of the Matrix Profile to the full cross-section. Comparing each segment’s nearest neighbors against a bootstrap random-walk benchmark yields the Pattern Distance Ratio, a measure of pattern recurrence.

Recurrence is genuine and persistent, and it predicts returns: a trajectory-space kernel ridge strategy earns annualized gross long–short alphas of roughly 9–15% across standard factor models. Charged at stock-level effective spreads, however, net alpha is indistinguishable from zero — the predictability is real but lives inside the no-arbitrage band transaction costs create. Transplanted with a fully frozen specification to fourteen international markets, the long–short return is positive in every market.

with C. Wei Li

CEO Individualism and the Distribution of Performance Risk

How do CEOs resolve the tradeoff between shareholder value and employee welfare when adverse shocks force an allocation choice? I measure CEO individualism from earnings-call speech in two steps: a transformer scores semantic similarity to 180 validated anchor sentences, and a specialized LLM — distilled from a frontier model’s judgments through QLoRA fine-tuning — isolates the high-exposure exchanges where CEOs must take a position without preparation.

Across roughly 376,000 quarterly calls for ExecuComp firms (2008–2024), a one-standard-deviation increase in individualism raises the pass-through of an ROA shock to employment by 6.2 percentage points. The effect is concentrated in downturns: individualistic CEOs cut workforces aggressively when performance falls but hire like collectivist peers in good times — a scarcity-revealed weighting channel whose asymmetry a symmetric efficiency story cannot generate.

When “Milliseconds” Matter: The Real Effects of High-Frequency Trading

We examine how high-frequency trading affects corporate investment and financing. Using a unique Australian setting with broker-level HFT identifiers, we exploit the introduction of ASX-ITCH — a high-speed order-level market data feed — as plausibly exogenous variation in HFT presence. As HFT activity rises, firms experience sizable declines in investment and equity issuance, concentrated among firms that rely more heavily on external equity financing. An alternative identification based on the staggered global rollout of exchange co-location services gives consistent evidence.

with Wesley Deng, Bo Hu, and Amy Kwan

The Protestant Ethic and the Principal-Agent Relation

Evidence from CEO employment agreements. We use large language models to extract and analyze CEO employment contracts from SEC filings for S&P 1500 firms, and find that firms headquartered in counties with higher Protestant adherence are less likely to use explicit CEO employment contracts — consistent with a cultural emphasis on situational judgment and implicit commitments over formal, codified agreements.

with C. Wei Li, Yuxuan Li, and Yiming Qian
