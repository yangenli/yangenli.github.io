Job Market Paper

Future Proves Past: Detecting Hidden Fraud under Imperfect Enforcement Labels

This paper asks whether SEC enforcement records can be used to find the fraud they never record. The occurrence and detection of fraud, while related, are not identical. Thus, a model trained on enforcement labels alone learns from a target shaped by the enforcement selection process and, by design, is not fully oriented toward identifying firms that commit fraud. Consequently, when a model correctly flags a firm the SEC never charged, standard accuracy counts it as a mistake.

Our solution starts from an economic idea: fraud borrows from the future. Accounting distortions tend to unwind, so manipulation often leaves traces in later accounting outcomes. Future outcomes do not prove fraud, so we use them only to adjust the training labels slightly. The method needs only a weak signal that improves the labels across a large sample. Because that signal comes from accounting outcomes rather than from more enforcement data, it also removes part of the SEC’s selection from what the model learns.

We train one model on these adjusted labels and an identical model on the raw enforcement labels, and test both only on firms the SEC never charged. Firms flagged by the adjusted model behave like hidden manipulators: they issue equity far more often, underperform for years afterward, and fewer of them have passed a bank’s due diligence. The model trained on raw enforcement labels alone shows little of the above pattern. The difference comes from the training target, not the model. Our approach shows that enforcement data can be supplemented to identify potential fraud that enforcement itself never detects.

with C. Wei Li, Yuxuan Li, and Anand M. Vijh

Listening to History Rhymes: Pattern Recurrence and Return Predictability in Stock Markets

This paper asks whether the shape of a stock’s recent return path repeats and predicts returns. We treat every recent return path of U.S. stocks over five decades as a shape, without naming any pattern in advance, and we measure how often each shape recurs relative to what a random walk would produce.

Recurrence is real and persistent across decades and countries. A strategy that trades on it earns sizable gross alpha after standard factors, but trading costs are large enough that no one can profit from it. The predictability is real, but it lives where trading does not pay. The paper is about how prices form, not about a trading rule.

CEO Individualism and the Distribution of Performance Risk

This paper asks how a person’s values, once that person runs a firm, turn into firm decisions. The value I study is individualism versus collectivism, and the decision is who bears the cost of poor performance, shareholders or employees. This tradeoff only matters in bad times. When a loss must fall on someone, the CEO’s priorities show.

I measure disposition from what CEOs say on earnings calls, by comparing their sentences with validated individualist and collectivist example sentences. I use only unscripted answers from the question-and-answer session, not prepared remarks that investor-relations staff review. Spontaneous answers are more likely to reveal the CEO’s underlying disposition, and this distinction is central to the measure’s credibility. For the same drop in performance, a CEO one standard deviation more individualistic than average cuts about twice as much employment. When performance rises, individualistic and collectivist CEOs hire alike. A CEO who simply adjusted labor faster would do so in both directions; a gap only in downturns points instead to different priorities.

The Protestant Ethic and the Principal-Agent Relation: Evidence from CEO Employment Agreements

This paper asks whether culture can substitute for formal contracts in governing a CEO. Weber’s thesis has a contracting implication: a culture that emphasizes work, personal responsibility, and internalized obligation may reduce the perceived need for explicit contractual control. We measure a firm’s exposure to this tradition by Protestant adherence in its headquarters county.

No comprehensive record of CEO employment agreements existed, so we built one by screening about a million EDGAR filings and classifying them with large language models. Firms in more Protestant counties are less likely to sign fixed-term employment agreements, and the contracts they do sign are thinner.

with C. Wei Li, Yuxuan Li, and Yiming Qian

When “Milliseconds” Matter: The Real Effects of High-Frequency Trading

This paper asks whether high-frequency trading affects firms’ investment and financing. Fast trading is hard to observe and moves with the fundamentals that drive investment. We therefore use Australian order-level data that identify fast traders directly and exploit an exchange upgrade that sped them up but left slower participants largely unaffected.

Firms more exposed to fast trading then invest less and issue less equity, their cost of equity rises, and liquidity does not improve. The evidence points to a financing-cost channel: market design can have real effects.

with Wesley Deng, Bo Hu, and Amy Kwan

Building a Contract Dataset with a Fine-Tuned Language Model, and What a Commercial Database Measures

This paper asks how to turn a million SEC filings into a dataset a researcher can trust. I fine-tune an open language model to read every exhibit filed since 2005 and say which ones are CEO employment contracts. Fine-tuning buys precision: the tuned model makes far fewer false calls than the best prompt. But a model that reads well is not yet a correct dataset. The mistakes that remain come from the code that links each document to a person and a year, not from reading.

So I propose a simple audit. Draw a sample, check it against an independent source, read every case twice, and plant cases whose answer is known. Applied to the leading commercial database, the same audit shows that its contract flag measures something different. Firms must file the contract, but they choose how to describe it. A measure built from filings sees the document. A measure built from proxy statements sees the description.

I fine-tune and distill open-weight LLMs (QLoRA) on a self-built three-GPU workstation with 88 GB of VRAM — all training and inference run locally, used to classify over one million SEC filings and to compute billion-scale trajectory-distance matrices.
