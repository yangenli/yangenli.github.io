Future Proves Past: Detecting Hidden Fraud under Imperfect Enforcement Labels

SEC enforcement labels are selective and incomplete proxies for the latent fraud they are meant to measure. That mismatch creates two problems: a model trained on them may learn the enforcement process rather than the fraud process, and accuracy measured against them cannot reveal whether a model detects the fraud enforcement misses.

We address both with a future-proves-past design. Because accounting distortions eventually reverse, future accounting outcomes modestly refine the training signal into soft labels; we train a convolutional neural network on multi-year accounting panels with these labels and validate it entirely outside the enforcement set. Out of sample over 2000–2019, flagged firms complete seasoned equity offerings at nearly three times the base rate and earn large negative post-issuance abnormal returns, a long–short portfolio earns significant factor-adjusted alpha, and externally screened populations — post-scrutiny Arthur Andersen clients, bank-monitored borrowers — are systematically avoided. An identically specified network trained on the enforcement label reproduces none of this: the training target drives the difference.

with C. Wei Li, Yuxuan Li, and Anand M. Vijh

Listening to History Rhymes: Pattern Recurrence and Return Predictability in Stock Markets

We embed rolling 60-day return segments of U.S. equities from 1970 to 2025 — roughly 1.32 million segments — into a geometric space of standardized trajectories, extending the exhaustive pairwise-comparison principle of the Matrix Profile to the full cross-section. Comparing each segment’s nearest neighbors against a bootstrap random-walk benchmark yields the Pattern Distance Ratio, a measure of pattern recurrence.

Recurrence is genuine and persistent — the in-sample/out-of-sample PDR correlation is 0.308 one year apart and still 0.270 three years apart, while a random-walk placebo produces a correlation roughly thirty times smaller — and it predicts returns: a trajectory-space kernel ridge strategy earns annualized gross long–short alphas of roughly 9–15% across standard factor models. Charged at stock-level effective spreads, however, net alpha is indistinguishable from zero — the predictability is real but lives inside the no-arbitrage band transaction costs create. Transplanted with a fully frozen specification to twelve international markets, the raw equal-weighted long–short return is positive in every market.

with C. Wei Li

CEO Individualism and the Distribution of Performance Risk

How do CEOs resolve the tradeoff between shareholder value and employee welfare when adverse shocks force an allocation choice? I measure CEO individualism from earnings-call speech in two steps: a transformer scores semantic similarity to 180 validated anchor sentences, and a specialized LLM — distilled from a frontier model’s judgments through QLoRA fine-tuning — isolates the high-exposure exchanges where CEOs must take a position without preparation.

Across roughly 376,000 quarterly calls for ExecuComp firms (2008–2024), a one-standard-deviation increase in individualism raises the pass-through of an ROA shock to employment by 6.2 percentage points. The effect is concentrated in downturns: individualistic CEOs cut workforces aggressively when performance falls but hire like collectivist peers in good times — a scarcity-revealed weighting channel whose asymmetry a symmetric efficiency story cannot generate.

When “Milliseconds” Matter: The Real Effects of High-Frequency Trading

We examine how high-frequency trading affects corporate investment and financing. Using a unique Australian setting with broker-level HFT identifiers, we exploit the introduction of ASX-ITCH — a high-speed order-level market data feed — as plausibly exogenous variation in HFT presence. As HFT activity rises, firms experience sizable declines in investment and equity issuance, concentrated among firms that rely more heavily on external equity financing. The staggered global rollout of exchange co-location services provides corroborating evidence.

with Wesley Deng, Bo Hu, and Amy Kwan

The Protestant Ethic and the Principal-Agent Relation

Evidence from CEO employment agreements. Using large language models to read exhibit filings on EDGAR, we assemble 6,373 explicit CEO employment contracts — 4,660 of them fixed-term agreements — covering 3,468 CEOs at 2,068 Execucomp firms from 2005 to 2022, to our knowledge the most comprehensive such sample assembled to date.

Firms headquartered in counties with higher Protestant adherence are markedly less likely to use fixed-term agreements, and agreements written there are not merely rarer but thinner on two alternative measures of contractual explicitness. The effect is denominational rather than devotional: total religious adherence carries no explanatory power, while Catholic adherence enters with the opposite sign.

with C. Wei Li, Yuxuan Li, and Yiming Qian
